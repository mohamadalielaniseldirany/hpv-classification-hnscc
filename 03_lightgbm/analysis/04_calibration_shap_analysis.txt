Analysis of Calibration, Probability Distributions, Feature Importance, and SHAP Results

This file continues the interpretation of the three LightGBM experiments:

1. Clinical-only
2. PET-only
3. Clinical + PET combined

The previous analysis focused mostly on validation and test metrics such as
ROC-AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. This file focuses on the other
outputs produced by the scripts:

- calibration table
- Expected Calibration Error
- probability distribution
- feature importance
- SHAP summary
- SHAP heatmap
- SHAP dependence plot


1. How to understand these outputs

Calibration table:
The calibration table groups patients by predicted probability. For example, the
bin [0.9, 1.0] contains patients that the model predicted with 90 percent to 100
percent probability of being HPV positive.

Ideal calibration:
The mean predicted probability should be close to the observed HPV positive rate.
For example, if a bin has mean predicted probability 0.90, then around 90 percent
of patients in that bin should truly be HPV positive.

Expected Calibration Error:
Expected Calibration Error, or ECE, summarizes the calibration table into one
number. Lower is better. The ideal value is 0.0.

Probability distribution:
This plot shows where the predicted probabilities are located for real HPV
negative and real HPV positive patients.

Ideal probability distribution:
HPV negative patients should be close to 0.0, and HPV positive patients should
be close to 1.0. A good model has less overlap between the two groups.

Feature importance:
Feature importance shows which variables LightGBM used most when building its
trees. In these scripts, gain importance is the main value. Gain means how much
a feature helped reduce model error when it was used.

Important note:
Feature importance does not prove that a feature causes HPV status. It only means
the model found that feature useful for prediction.

SHAP summary:
SHAP explains how much each feature pushes predictions up or down. In this
project, pushing up means increasing the HPV-positive score. Pushing down means
decreasing the HPV-positive score.

SHAP heatmap:
The SHAP heatmap shows feature effects patient by patient. It helps show whether
the same features affect many patients, or whether different patients are
controlled by different features.

SHAP dependence:
The SHAP dependence plot focuses on one important feature and shows how that
feature changes the model prediction.

Ideal SHAP result:
There is no perfect SHAP number. The goal is to see whether the model is using
reasonable and stable patterns, and whether a few features are dominating too
strongly.


2. Clinical-only experiment

Input:
The clinical-only model used 8 clinical features:

- CenterID
- Age
- Gender
- Tobacco Consumption
- Alcohol Consumption
- Performance Status
- Treatment
- M-stage


2.1 Clinical-only calibration table and ECE

Validation ECE:
0.071

Test ECE:
0.085

Ideal ECE:
0.000

Interpretation:
The clinical-only model had fairly good calibration, but it was not perfect. The
test ECE was slightly worse than validation, which means the probabilities were a
little less reliable on the test set.

Validation calibration details:

- In the [0.9, 1.0] bin, there were 67 patients.
- The mean predicted probability was 0.993.
- The observed HPV positive rate was 0.955.
- The gap was only 0.038.

This is good for the high-probability group. Most patients that the model called
very likely HPV positive were truly HPV positive.

However, some smaller bins had large gaps. For example:

- The [0.1, 0.2] bin had 1 patient.
- Mean predicted probability was 0.134.
- Observed HPV positive rate was 1.000.
- Gap was 0.866.

This means that one patient was predicted with low HPV-positive probability but
was actually HPV positive. Because the bin had only 1 patient, the gap looks very
large.

Test calibration details:

- In the [0.9, 1.0] bin, there were 67 patients.
- The mean predicted probability was 0.994.
- The observed HPV positive rate was 0.955.
- The gap was 0.039.

Again, the high-probability group was well calibrated.

But the lower probability bins showed mistakes:

- The [0.1, 0.2] bin had 4 patients with observed HPV positive rate 0.750.
- The [0.2, 0.3] bin had 1 patient with observed HPV positive rate 1.000.

This tells us that some true HPV positive patients received low probabilities.

So the plot probably shows a clear cluster of HPV positive patients near 1.0,

2.6 Clinical-only SHAP dependence

M-stage

On the test set:

3. PET-only experiment

Input:
The PET-only model used 107 PET radiomics features.


3.1 PET-only calibration table and ECE

Validation ECE:
0.087

Test ECE:
0.130

Ideal ECE:
0.000

Interpretation:
The PET-only model had worse calibration than the clinical-only model, especially
on the test set. The test ECE increased to 0.130, meaning the PET-only predicted
probabilities were less reliable.

Validation calibration details:

- In the [0.9, 1.0] bin, there were 67 patients.
- Mean predicted probability was 0.995.
- Observed HPV positive rate was 0.955.
- Gap was 0.040.

This high-probability bin was not bad. But the smaller bins had unstable and
large gaps, because they contained very few patients.

Test calibration details:

- In the [0.9, 1.0] bin, there were 70 patients.
- Mean predicted probability was 0.997.
- Observed HPV positive rate was 0.900.
- Gap was 0.097.

This is important. The PET-only model put 70 out of 78 test patients in the very
high probability bin, but only 90 percent of them were truly HPV positive. That
means the model was too confident in predicting HPV positive.

The biggest warning sign:

- The [0.0, 0.1] test bin had 1 patient.
- Mean predicted probability was 0.027.
- Observed HPV positive rate was 1.000.
- Gap was 0.973.

This means one true HPV positive patient was predicted as almost certainly HPV
negative.

5. Final comparison across the three experiments

Calibration:

- Clinical-only test ECE: 0.085
- PET-only test ECE: 0.130
- Combined test ECE: 0.069

Best calibration:
The combined model had the best test calibration because it had the lowest ECE.

Probability distributions:

- Clinical-only separated HPV positives well, but some negatives overlapped with
  positives.
- PET-only gave high HPV-positive probabilities to almost everyone, including
  most HPV negative patients.
- Combined separated HPV positives very well and improved the negative group
  compared with PET-only, but still misclassified some negatives.

Feature importance:

- Clinical-only was dominated by M-stage, Age, and Tobacco Consumption.
- PET-only used shape and texture radiomics features, especially
  Maximum2DDiameterSlice.
- Combined was still dominated by clinical features, especially M-stage, but PET
  shape features appeared in the top 10.

SHAP summary:

- Clinical-only: M-stage was the strongest patient-level contributor.
- PET-only: Maximum2DDiameterSlice and Sphericity were the strongest PET
  contributors.
- Combined: M-stage remained strongest, followed by Tobacco Consumption, CenterID,
  and several PET shape features.

SHAP heatmap:

- Clinical-only heatmap showed a few clinical features driving most predictions.
- PET-only heatmap showed several radiomics features affecting predictions, but
  not enough clean separation between classes.
- Combined heatmap showed clinical features driving the main prediction and PET
  features adding patient-specific adjustments.

SHAP dependence:

- Clinical-only dependence: M-stage was very influential.
- PET-only dependence: Maximum2DDiameterSlice had a strong negative relationship
  with its SHAP value.
- Combined dependence: M-stage stayed very influential even after PET features
  were added.


6. Simple final conclusion

The extra plots and tables confirm the same general story as the metrics.

The clinical-only model was strong because a few clinical features, especially
M-stage, carried important predictive information.

The PET-only model found important radiomics features, especially tumor shape and
texture features, but its probability distribution showed a major problem: it
predicted most patients as HPV positive, including most HPV negative patients.

The combined model gave the best overall test behavior. It had the best test ECE,
strong probability separation for HPV positive patients, and useful top features
from both clinical and PET data. However, the interpretation plots show that the
model was still mainly driven by clinical features, especially M-stage, while PET
features added extra but smaller adjustments.

So the main interpretation is:

Clinical features gave the strongest stable signal. PET features alone were not
enough. Combining clinical and PET features improved several final results, but
the combined model still relied heavily on the clinical information.
